About

Biography

I am a Postdoctoral Researcher at Arizona State University, working with Prof. Krishnendu Chakrabarty (IEEE, ACM, and AAAS Fellow).

My research starts from one premise: hardware and extreme environments can silently change what a model does, what it prefers, and what it reveals. I work on AI-driven and agentic design automation and test, hardware/software co-design (from learning methods and model architectures to mapping and hardware architectures), self-aware hardware, and hardware security for foundation models and agentic AI.

At ASU, I work on two space AI projects: PHOENIX, funded by the National Reconnaissance Office (NRO), and SLEAC, a DoD Microelectronics Commons project. Both develop low-energy, memory-centric AI accelerators for space and remote sensing, in collaboration with Sandia National Laboratories and Alphacore. I also advised an Advantest-funded program on agentic AI for semiconductor test. I have co-written several research proposals with my PIs in the US and Europe, both funded and unfunded.

I received my Dr.-Ing. (PhD) in Computer Science summa cum laude from the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT), Germany, in 2024, advised by Prof. Mehdi B. Tahoori (IEEE Fellow) and Prof. Krishnendu Chakrabarty. My dissertation was nominated for the EDAA Outstanding Dissertation Award and was a semi-finalist in the TTTC E. J. McCluskey Best Doctoral Thesis Competition. At KIT, I led the four-year research agenda of the French–German NeuSPIN project with SPINTEC, CEA, and Université Grenoble Alpes.

Before my PhD, I worked at Infineon Technologies and Robert Bosch GmbH. I also co-founded two ventures: MemoryCentric.AI (LLM optimization) and MeinTV at UnternehmerTUM.

Research

TRYGG Computing Lab

trygg: safe, secure. Aligned, safe, secure, and reliable AI on emerging hardware, built for the environments where it is deployed.

My research targets the alignment, safety, security, and reliability of generative and agentic AI deployed on emerging AI hardware in extreme environments. Foundation models increasingly run on compute-in-memory, neuromorphic, photonic, and chiplet-based accelerators, and in places where conventional electronics struggle, such as orbit and deep space.

In these settings, radiation, temperature swings, aging, shared hardware, and analog non-idealities can quietly change what a model does, what it prefers, and what it reveals, often without any visible alarm and with little or no chance of physical repair. My goal is AI systems whose behavior can be measured, trusted, and maintained across the full stack, from devices and accelerator architectures to mapping, learning methods, and models.

Where AI must keep working: extreme environments

Current focus

Space, deep space & satellite remote sensing

  • On-board AI for remote sensing: low-energy, reliable memory-centric AI accelerators for remote-sensing workloads in orbit (NRO PHOENIX)
  • Radiation-hard AI chips: energy-efficient AI chips integrated with focal-plane-array image sensors for on-satellite inference (DoD Microelectronics Commons)
  • Space data centers: resilient and secure computing infrastructure beyond Earth (COINS 2026, FPT 2026)

Stresses: cosmic rays and solar particle events (single-event upsets, total-ionizing dose), swings from −250 °F to +250 °F, vacuum with no convective cooling, tight power budgets, and limited downlink that forces AI to run on board

Constrained communication: latency and bandwidth limits mean AI has to run at the edge instead of sending raw data home

No maintenance access: systems run unattended for years, so reliability matters more than peak performance

A costly toolbox: conventional fixes (radiation-hardened silicon, ECC, triple-modular redundancy) are expensive for AI accelerators, so my work develops AI-aware, low-overhead alternatives

PhD Dissertation · KIT 2024 · Summa cum laude

Scalable and Efficient Uncertainty Estimation and Reduction in Edge-AI Accelerators

Referees: Prof. Dr. Mehdi B. Tahoori (KIT) and Prof. Dr. Krishnendu Chakrabarty (ASU) · EDAA Outstanding Dissertation Award nominee · TTTC E. J. McCluskey Best Doctoral Thesis semi-finalist

This thesis makes uncertainty estimation and reduction practical on resource-constrained edge AI accelerators. It covers predictive uncertainty estimation, functional uncertainty of the accelerator itself, reduction of uncertainty from hardware non-idealities, and continuous availability. It does so through algorithm–hardware co-design: problem-aware training, novel normalization and dropout methods, hardware architectures, compact test generation, and online testing frameworks.

How I teach

  • Working, demonstrated systems carry the largest share of the grade, alongside individual design rationales and failure analyses
  • Projects span the full stack, so students reason about the model, the software, and the hardware constraints together
  • Professional practice from week one: Git, code review, automated tests, and CI
  • Course content is refreshed every offering to reflect where the field is now

Teaching interests

I am prepared to teach undergraduate and graduate courses in digital design, computer organization and systems programming, VLSI design, VLSI verification and test, hardware security and trust, and machine learning hardware, and to advise capstone teams.

New course: Secure, Dependable, and Aligned AI Systems

A graduate elective (open to senior undergraduates) covering uncertainty estimation and calibration; robustness, alignment, and evaluation of LLMs and agentic AI; non-idealities and silent data corruption in compute-in-memory and photonic accelerators; testing and fault tolerance of AI accelerators; and attacks on deployed models (fault injection, side channels, data remanence). It is built around one staged, reproducible project on public models, and I will release the materials openly.
